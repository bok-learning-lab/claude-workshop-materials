# Source → Teaching Materials

The input is a primary source — a single dense paper, a long-form transcript, a lecturer's plain-text notes, a folder of candidate readings. The output is something the instructor uses in class or sends to students: a case and discussion plan, a Top-10 takeaways doc, an illustrated lecture page with verified catalogue facts, a navigable hub of paper summaries. The artifacts differ; the pipeline shape is the same. The model does the work that scales poorly — drafting a case that isolates one move in an argument, ranking ten things out of a ninety-minute conversation, resolving twelve `SHOW:` markers against a museum's catalogue, reading a stack of PDFs end-to-end so the instructor can decide which are load-bearing for next week's seminar. The instructor stays the moral agent: the decider, the rememberer of what landed in the room, the one who knows.

The discipline that recurs across these projects is **two-pass**. Write the neutral summary first; write the pedagogical twist second; keep them visually separate in the artifact so a reader can consume the description without the editorial layer. Without that structural separation the agenda leaks into the description. This is most explicit in **research-helper** — the prompt is structured so the faithful summary and the agenda-bridging "twist" are separate passes, with the instruction to say *"this does not transfer"* when that is the truthful reading. It is the same move in **paper-to-teaching**: a `/teaching-case` writes a student-facing case first; a separate set of instructor notes ties each move back to a section of the source paper. Speculative pedagogical connections are marked as speculative. Factual claims about real-world objects (a painting's date, a paper's citation) are fetched from the institution that owns them, not improvised from the model's memory.

## Projects in this category

- **[Paper to teaching materials](paper-to-teaching/)** — turns Grant, Behrends & Basl's 2025 *Philosophical Studies* paper on automated decision-making into a four-skill toolkit (`/teaching-case`, `/discussion-plan`, `/objection-audit`, `/quiz`) for a graduate seminar. The self-referential point: the paper is precisely about the limits of relying on AI for consequential decisions about people. The project models the relationship the paper *prescribes*.
- **[Class summarizer](class-summarizer/)** — the meta-project. This is the pipeline that produced the daily Top-10 takeaways docs faculty have been reading every morning of the workshop. Forced count of ten, bold one-sentence headlines, italic provenance opening, quotes verbatim. Read the full transcript before writing anything; rank ruthlessly.
- **[Art history lecture](art-history-lecture/)** — the MCP example. A lecturer's plain-text notes with twelve `SHOW:` markers become a single illustrated web page where every image streams live from Harvard Art Museums' IIIF service and every catalogue fact is fetched, not improvised.
- **[Research helper](research-helper/)** — turns a folder of arXiv-style PDFs into one self-contained HTML summary per paper plus a hub `index.html`. The two-pass structure (neutral summary → honest twist, visually separated) is the structural move that keeps the agenda from leaking into the description.
- **Course preparation** — the *before-class* counterpart to the class summarizer, from the June 8–11 cohort. Two worked paths: source-grounded curricular recommendations built on four reference layers (the Bok Center's Recentering Academics guidance, Harvard's institutional grading reports, the external research base, practitioner guidance), with a `/syllabus-redesign` skill as the canonical move; and a lecture-notes pipeline that merges blackboard content (read from video frames with vision) with the spoken transcript into a three-layer document students can use. Source: [`claude-code-20260610/examples/course-preparation/`](https://github.com/bok-learning-lab/claude-code-20260610/tree/main/examples/course-preparation).
- **Admin email drafter** — the same source-grounded discipline pointed at advising mail, from the June 8–11 cohort. A `/draft-reply` skill answers a student's program-requirements questions strictly from the official requirements documents, citing every claim, writing in the administrator's own voice, and explicitly flagging the line between "the requirements say X" and "confirm this at an advising meeting." Claude drafts; the administrator reviews and sends. Source: [`claude-code-20260611/examples/admin-email-drafter/`](https://github.com/bok-learning-lab/claude-code-20260611/tree/main/examples/admin-email-drafter).
